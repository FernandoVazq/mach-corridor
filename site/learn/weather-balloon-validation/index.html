What 3,500 weather balloons said about the forecast

A forecast is only useful if you know how wrong it is. To find out, I compared the boomless Mach that the models predicted with the one measured by weather balloons, at 12 stations, twice a day, for a full year.

By Fernando J. Vázquez · Updated 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

Why balloons

Twice a day, at hundreds of stations around the world, meteorologists release a balloon carrying a small instrument called a radiosonde. As it rises to 30 km or more it radios back temperature, humidity and wind. It is the closest thing we have to a direct measurement of the whole column of air that a boom travels through, and the records are public in NOAA's IGRA2 archive.

For each launch I computed the boomless Mach from the balloon's own profile, using exactly the same physics as the map, and treated it as the truth. Then I computed it from the GFS and ECMWF forecasts for the same place and time, from the analysis (the model's best picture of the present) out to 24 hours ahead. The difference is the forecast error.

The year ran from October 2025 to September 2026: about 3,500 launches at 12 stations in the United States, Alaska and Europe, including three in the Rocky Mountains, checked at 35,000, 45,000 and 55,000 ft and in every direction.

How big the error is

The error is measured as a root mean square (RMS), a typical size of the miss in Mach. With GFS alone it is 0.0104 at the analysis, 0.0113 six hours ahead and 0.0130 a day ahead. With the average of GFS and ECMWF it drops to 0.0092, 0.0104 and 0.0117. For an airplane at 55,000 ft, 0.0117 Mach is about 7 knots.

Two details matter more than the headline. First, the error is not symmetric. On average the forecast is slightly slower than the balloon (a bias of −0.002 to −0.003), which is the safe direction. The error on the unsafe side, where the model allows more speed than the air really does, is 0.0095 a day ahead for the two-model average.

Second, most of what is left is not forecast skill at all. Even at the analysis, when the model knows the present as well as it can, the error is 0.009. That is the difference between a 1° box of the model, 100 km wide, and one balloon at one point inside it. A finer 0.25° version of GFS did not help, which says the same thing.

Where it is harder

The stations do not all look the same. Over the Rocky Mountains the day-ahead error is about 20 % larger than on the plains, because the model smooths the terrain and the air around it. Europe's two stations are the easiest. This is why the margin uses a larger unseen error over mountains (0.015 instead of 0.012).

From error to safety: a year of past weather

Knowing the error is half the job. The other half is asking what it does to real flights. For that I replayed a year of past weather: 53 weekly forecasts, 48 routes in both directions, an airplane like Boom's Overture at 55,000 ft, and a realistic extra error added to the atmosphere each day, correlated over hundreds of kilometres as real errors are.

With a perfect forecast, corridors would save 36 minutes per flight against today's rule of slowing to subsonic over all land. With the real forecast and its margin they save about 25 to 28 minutes, and an estimated 0.28 % of flights would put more than the proposed limit of 0.11 psf on the ground somewhere along the route (95 % upper bound 0.47 %, against a design target of 0.5 %). Flying a fixed Mach 1.05 and ignoring the weather saves only 19 minutes and still lets the boom reach the ground on 1.1 % of the distance.

The difference between 28 and 36 minutes is the price of not knowing the atmosphere exactly. It is also a measure of what better forecasts are worth: in the hindcast, shaving 0.02 Mach off the margin without a better forecast multiplied the flights with a boom on land 37 times.

Two practical lessons

The year of balloons also gave two findings that change how the forecast should be used, not just how good it is. The first is that timing matters: re-planning with the forecast issued about six hours before departure, instead of the one from the day before, cuts the error by 11 to 14 %. That is why the route planner warns when the forecast is getting old and switches corridors off once it is more than 18 hours old.

The second is that the two models are most useful when they disagree. On the 10 % of samples where GFS and ECMWF differed the most, the day-ahead error was 0.0152, against 0.0108 on the quarter where they agreed best. The map can hatch those places, so you can see at a glance where to trust it less. That signal does not yet enter the margin, because adding it properly needs a hindcast with both models and the ensemble on the same dates, which I have not built yet.

What is still not proven

I set myself a target at the start: an error of 0.010 Mach. Honestly, it is not met as a plain RMS by any single model, even at the analysis. The two-model average meets it only when you count the unsafe side, which I think is the right way to count, but that is a choice, and I have left it marked as open.

One year of weekly dates is also not enough to prove a 0.5 % rate with confidence: the 95 % upper bounds for some levels and policies are between 0.9 % and 1.9 %. And the loudness side of the problem, how loud the ground gets just below the turning point, rests on a small set of NASA flight tests. Microphone data from a real Mach-cutoff flight such as XB-1's would be the most valuable next check.

Every number in this article is recomputed by code in the project's repository, and the validation runs again every month in CI, so these figures will change as more data comes in.
