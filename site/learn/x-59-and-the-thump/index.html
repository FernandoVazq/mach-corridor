NASA's X-59 and the "thump"

There are two ways to keep a supersonic jet from disturbing the people below. One is to fly slowly enough that the boom never lands. The other is to build an airplane whose boom, when it lands, is barely a sound at all. NASA's X-59 is the second idea, flying.

By Fernando J. Vázquez · Updated 4 November 2026 · 6 min read

An airplane shaped around its sound

The X-59 is a one-seat research jet built by Lockheed Martin for NASA's Quesst mission. It is 99.7 ft long, much of it nose, and designed to cruise at about Mach 1.4 at 55,000 ft. Everything about its shape serves one goal: to stop the many small shock waves from the nose, the cockpit, the wings, the engine and the tail from merging into the two strong shocks that make a classic boom.

A normal supersonic airplane makes an "N-wave": the pressure jumps up suddenly at the front, falls steadily, and jumps back at the end. Your ear hears the two jumps as the double bang. The X-59's long nose, its small canards and its engine mounted on top of the fuselage spread the shocks out so they arrive as gentler ramps. The sound that reaches the ground is meant to be a soft thump, which NASA has compared to a car door closing down the street. The design goal is about 75 PLdB, a perceived-loudness scale on which Concorde's boom rated far higher.

The long nose has a famous side effect: the pilot cannot see straight ahead. The X-59 has no forward window and uses a camera system, NASA's External Vision System, shown on a screen in the cockpit.

Where the flight tests stand

The X-59 first flew on 28 October 2025, a subsonic hop from Palmdale to NASA's Armstrong Flight Research Center at Edwards, California. It went supersonic for the first time on 5 June 2026, at about Mach 1.1, and soon after reached Mach 1.4. By late October 2026 it had flown more than 40 times, above 56,000 ft and up to Mach 1.53, and NASA was starting the acoustic phase: measuring on the ground what it actually sounds like.

NASA describes Quesst in three phases: building and flying the airplane, then acoustic validation, then community response. The order matters. Before anyone asks a town how a thump felt, the team has to show that the airplane makes the thump it was designed to make, in real air, measured on the ground and matched to the predictions. Only then do the community surveys mean something.

After that come community flights: flying over towns that agree to take part and asking residents what they heard. Those answers are meant to give the FAA and the international aviation body, ICAO, real data on how much boom people accept, which is exactly the question the 1964 Oklahoma City tests tried to answer with a far louder airplane.

The measurements themselves are made the way NASA has measured booms for decades: lines of microphones laid out on the ground across the flight path, recording the pressure wave as it arrives, together with weather balloons released near the test so the atmosphere of that hour is known. Without the balloon, a quiet boom on a hot day and a loud one on a cold day could look like two different airplanes.

The carpet, and why it depends on the weather

Even a quiet boom has to land somewhere. The strip of ground where the X-59's thump is heard, its carpet, runs along the flight path, and its width is set by the same physics as Mach cutoff. Straight below the airplane the boom always reaches the ground. Off to the sides it travels at a slant through warmer air near the ground, bends upward, and far enough out it turns back before landing. That turning point is the edge of the carpet.

So the carpet changes from day to day. A hot afternoon, or flying into a strong wind, narrows it; cold air and a tailwind widen it. On the day I wrote this, the forecast over Edwards gave about 45 to 56 km depending on the direction of flight, against about 127 km in a standard atmosphere. For community tests that matters a lot: it decides which towns hear the airplane and which do not. The X-59 page on this site forecasts that width for the next 48 hours.

The same forecast can help plan the tests themselves. If a community flight is meant to put the thump over a particular town, the airplane's track has to be chosen with that day's carpet in mind: on a narrow-carpet day a town 25 km off the track may hear nothing, and on a wide one a town 60 km away may hear it. Knowing that in advance is the difference between a useful survey and a wasted flight.

Two answers to one problem

Shaping and Mach cutoff are often presented as rivals. I think they work best together. A shaped airplane makes the boom quiet when it lands; Mach cutoff keeps it from landing at all, but only up to the speed the weather allows. A future airliner could use both: fly boomless when the day allows a useful speed, and rely on a quieter signature when it does not, staying under a limit like the FAA's proposed 0.11 psf either way.

There is also a limit to what a forecast like mine can say about the X-59. It gives where the thump is heard, not how loud: its shaped signature needs NASA's own data. And it is an independent project, not affiliated with NASA; the test routes, dates and towns are NASA's to announce.
