How the Mach Corridor forecast works

Every six hours the world's weather services publish a new forecast of the atmosphere. Within about an hour, Mach Corridor turns it into a map of how fast a supersonic jet can fly without its boom reaching the ground. Here is what happens in between.

By Fernando J. Vázquez · Updated 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

The data: two free global forecasts

The forecast starts from two weather models that anyone can download. The first is NOAA's Global Forecast System (GFS), the American model, on a 1° grid (about 100 km) with temperature, humidity, wind and height on 23 pressure levels from near the ground up to about 20 km, every 6 hours for the next 48 hours. The second is the ECMWF IFS, the European model, which publishes an open-data version under a Creative Commons licence. I thin it to the same 1° grid.

Using two models was the single largest improvement in the whole project. Against weather balloons, the average of the two is more accurate than either one alone (the details are in the validation article). Where either model says there is no corridor, the map shows none.

A third source sets the safety margin: NOAA's GEFS ensemble, 31 versions of the same forecast started from slightly different conditions. Where the 31 members disagree, the forecast is less certain, and the margin grows.

What is computed, point by point

For every 1° point of the globe, the build takes the column of air above it and does the same few steps.

Speed of sound at each height. Sound speed depends on temperature and, a little, on humidity, so the build computes it from both. An early version used a shortcut that made hot, humid air slightly too fast; it was fixed in model 2.10, and the mistake is listed in the change log.

The wind along each direction. The airplane could fly in any direction, so the build repeats the calculation for 36 headings, every 10°. For each heading it adds the wind component along the boom's path to the sound speed, and it also checks rays leaving the cone up to 45° to the side, because a crosswind can make a side ray the critical one.

The floor. The boom must turn back above the highest ground under it, not above sea level. The model's own terrain is smoothed, so the build raises it to the highest point of a 600 m elevation model under the boom's footprint, then adds a 1 km buffer.

The limit. The highest Mach at which every ray turns above the floor. This is the number behind the colours on the map, for seven flight levels between 30,000 and 60,000 ft, plus estimates up to 100,000 ft that are not yet checked against balloons.

The margin: how much to hold back

A forecast is never exact, so the speed you would actually fly is the forecast limit minus a margin. The margin is the most important safety decision in the project, and I tried to set it from evidence rather than from a round number.

At each point and direction it combines three things: the spread of the 31 GEFS members, an error that no model run can see (about 0.012 Mach, measured against balloons, raised to 0.015 over mountains), and an allowance for how precisely an airplane holds its Mach (0.005). These are added the way independent errors are, and multiplied by a factor of about 4. A typical margin ends up near 0.05 Mach.

The factor was fitted on a year of past weather so that fewer than 0.5 % of flights would put more than 0.11 psf on the ground, and then tested on months it had not seen. The validation article explains how that test went, including where it is still weak.

From numbers to a map in your browser

All of this runs on GitHub Actions, the free automation service of the site's code host. Every hour a small job checks whether a newer complete GFS run exists. When it does, the full build starts: download, physics, margin, rocket and sound forecasts, and a suite of tests. GFS starts a new run four times a day, at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC, and the data are complete a few hours later, so the map you see is usually between 4 and 10 hours old. The tests check the ray tracer against exact textbook solutions and the loudness model against published cases, and a browser test plus an accessibility audit open the site before anything is published. If a test fails, the old map stays up.

The results are packed into small images in which each pixel's brightness is a number, one tile per direction and level. Your browser downloads them and draws the map, so the site itself has no server to run and costs only the domain, about $11 a year. The route planner, the 3D view and the rocket and sound pages all read the same files.

Rules that fail safe

A planning tool should say "no" when it does not know. The route planner turns corridors off and flies subsonic over land when the forecast is older than 18 hours, when a leg would end past the end of the forecast, or when the margin cannot be computed. Every plan can be downloaded as an evidence file with the data runs, the model version and a checksum, so anyone can check where each number came from.

What it does not do

It is a research project, not an approved flight-planning system. It treats the air above each point as one column; a check against a full 3D ray tracer found the two agree within about 0.002 Mach in a typical case. It does not model the extra-loud focused booms made when an airplane turns or accelerates, and its loudness estimates below the turning point rest on a small set of flight tests. Those limits are listed in full, with what would fix each one, in the method document.
