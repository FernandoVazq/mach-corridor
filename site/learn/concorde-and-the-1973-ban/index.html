Why Concorde could not fly supersonic over land

Concorde could cross the Atlantic in about three and a half hours, yet over land it had to fly slower than a regular jet. The reason goes back to six months in 1964 when a city in Oklahoma was boomed eight times a day.

By Fernando J. Vázquez · Updated 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

The race for a supersonic airliner

In the 1960s it looked obvious that the next airliners would be supersonic. Britain and France joined forces on Concorde, the Soviet Union built the Tu-144, and in the United States the government funded Boeing to design an even larger and faster airliner, the 2707. The open question was not whether these airplanes could fly, but whether people on the ground would accept their booms.

Six months of booms in Oklahoma City

To find out, the US government ran an experiment that is hard to imagine today. From 3 February to 29 July 1964, in a test known as Operation Bongo II, Air Force jets flew supersonic over Oklahoma City on a schedule, about eight times a day, 1,253 booms in all, across a strip about 16 miles wide.

The results were mixed in a way that decided the future. In the surveys most residents, 73 %, said they could live with the booms. But a quarter said they could not, roughly 15,000 people wrote, called, sued or protested, and the city logged nearly 10,000 complaints of damage, mostly cracked plaster and broken glass, including 147 windows in two of its tallest buildings in the first 14 weeks. A loud quarter of a city is enough to sink a technology.

The ban

Congress cut off funding for the American supersonic transport in 1971, and Boeing's 2707 was never built. In 1973 the Federal Aviation Administration banned civil aircraft from flying faster than Mach 1 over the United States. The rule, today 14 CFR 91.817, does not set a noise limit. It simply forbids the speed, whatever the airplane and whatever the weather. In 1978 it was extended to cover booms that would reach US soil from aircraft flying outside it.

That detail, a ban on speed rather than on noise, is the reason Mach cutoff never mattered for half a century. Even an airplane whose boom would never reach the ground was not allowed to try.

Concorde: Mach 2, but only over water

Concorde entered service in January 1976 and flew passengers until 2003. It cruised at about Mach 2 near 55,000 to 60,000 ft, and its boom was loud: about 2 psf on the ground, a figure the loudness model in this project reproduces from NASA's published measurement within 15 %. So its routes were built around the oceans. London and Paris to New York and Washington were its signature flights, supersonic over the Atlantic and subsonic over land at both ends.

How loud is 2 psf? Indoors it rattles windows and makes people jump; outdoors it is a sharp double crack that can be heard tens of kilometres to each side of the flight path. A booster landing at Cape Canaveral, measured for the FAA 8.9 km away, put 1.6 to 1.7 psf on the ground, which gives a sense of what living under a Concorde route would have meant several times a day.

That limit shaped its economics. A transatlantic route was one of the few where supersonic flight covered most of the trip. Over continents the airplane would have spent much of each flight at subsonic speed while burning far more fuel than a regular jet. Only 14 Concordes ever entered airline service, with British Airways and Air France, and after a fatal accident in 2000 and a collapse in premium travel the fleet was retired in 2003.

What changed after fifty years

Three things changed at once. In January 2025 Boom's XB-1 flew supersonic over the Mojave Desert with no boom heard on the ground, using Mach cutoff. In June 2025 an executive order, "Leading the World in Supersonic Flight", directed the FAA to replace the speed ban. And on 2 July 2026 the FAA proposed doing exactly that: repeal the ban in 91.817 and replace it with a noise limit of 0.11 psf at the surface, for the main boom and for the secondary booms that the stratosphere can bend back to the ground far away. Comments closed on 17 August 2026.

A limit of 0.11 psf is about one twentieth of Concorde's boom. A quiet-boom design such as NASA's X-59 aims to get close to it by shaping the airplane. Mach cutoff aims to get under it by not letting the boom reach the ground at all, at the cost of flying only as fast as the day's weather allows.

To make that concrete: on one day in October 2026, the route planner on this site flew a Concorde-like airplane from New York to Los Angeles at 55,000 ft. Following the boomless corridors over land took 4 hours 9 minutes; obeying today's rule, supersonic only over water and subsonic across the whole country, took 4 hours 38 minutes. Twenty-nine minutes is not Mach 2, but it is the first time in fifty years that the gap could be legal.

Why I find this history useful

It is tempting to read the 1973 ban as a mistake. I do not think it was. In 1964 nobody could forecast where a boom would land, and the airplanes of the time could not choose a speed that kept it in the sky. The new proposal works because both of those things are now possible: we can predict the atmosphere well enough, and an airplane can fly a precise Mach. That is the gap Mach Corridor tries to fill, in the open, so anyone can check the numbers.
