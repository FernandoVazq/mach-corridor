What Mach cutoff is, and why a sonic boom sometimes never reaches the ground

On 28 January 2025 Boom's XB-1 flew faster than sound over the Mojave Desert, and the microphones on the ground heard nothing. It was not a trick of the airplane. It was the air.

By Fernando J. Vázquez · Updated 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

I started Mach Corridor because of that flight. I wanted to know how often the same thing could happen, where, and at what speed. Before any of the forecasts make sense, though, it helps to see why a boom can stay in the sky at all. This article is the explanation I wish I had read first: no equations until the very end, and then only one.

A boom is not a single bang

An airplane flying faster than sound pushes the air ahead of it faster than the air can move out of the way. The pressure piles up into shock waves that trail behind the airplane in a cone, like the wake behind a boat. Wherever that cone sweeps across the ground, people hear a sharp double bang: the sonic boom.

Two things surprise most people. First, the boom is not made once, at the moment the airplane "breaks the sound barrier". It is made the whole time the airplane flies supersonic, so it drags a strip of noise along the entire route, often tens of kilometres wide. Second, it is loud. Concorde at Mach 2 put about 2 pounds per square foot (psf) of overpressure on the ground. That is like close thunder, and it is the reason the United States banned civil supersonic flight over land in 1973.

Sound bends toward slower air

The shock cone is made of sound, and sound does not always travel in straight lines. It bends, the same way light bends when it passes from air into water. The rule is simple: sound turns toward the air where it travels more slowly.

Sound is faster in warm air than in cold air. In the lower atmosphere the air usually gets colder with height, about 6.5 °C for every kilometre, until the tropopause near 11 km, where the cooling stops. So at 55,000 ft (about 17 km) the air is around −56 °C and sound moves at roughly 295 m/s, while near the ground at 15 °C it moves at about 340 m/s.

Now follow a piece of the boom on its way down. It starts in cold, slow air and travels into warmer, faster air. Because it keeps turning toward the slower air above it, its path curves away from the ground. If the airplane is flying slowly enough, the path flattens out before it reaches the ground and turns back up. The boom never lands. That is Mach cutoff.

The rule that decides it

There is a neat way to say all of this with one comparison. Look at how fast the airplane moves over the ground. Then look at the fastest speed of sound anywhere between the airplane and the ground. If the airplane is slower, every part of the boom turns back up before it lands. If the airplane is faster, some of it reaches the ground.

With no wind and a standard atmosphere, that gives a limit of about Mach 1.15 for an airplane above the tropopause: 340 divided by 295. Fly at Mach 1.10 and the boom stays up; fly at Mach 1.20 and it lands. In the real atmosphere the limit moves every day, between roughly Mach 1.0 and 1.3, and that movement is what the map on this site forecasts.

Wind matters more than temperature

The "speed of sound" that matters here is the sound speed plus the wind along the direction of flight. A tailwind at cruise altitude makes the airplane faster over the ground without making the sound near the ground any faster, so it lowers the boomless Mach. A wind blowing the same way near the ground speeds up the sound there, so it raises it. Only the difference between the two counts: a wind that blows the same at every height changes nothing.

That is why direction matters as much as the weather. In the same air, the best and the worst direction of flight differ by about 0.09 Mach on a typical day at 55,000 ft. On the map the small arrows of the Best view point into the wind for exactly this reason.

Altitude helps, terrain costs a little

Flying higher puts more of the atmosphere between the airplane and the ground and makes the comparison more favourable. On a year of weather balloons the median limit rises from Mach 1.058 at 35,000 ft to Mach 1.144 at 55,000 ft, and with a small margin of 0.03 a usable corridor exists on 93 % of soundings at 55,000 ft against 38 % at 35,000 ft. Above about 55,000 ft the gain is small, because the air up there no longer gets colder.

The ground itself matters too. The boom has to turn back before it reaches the highest terrain under the airplane, not sea level, so mountains raise the floor. In the forecast I keep the turning point at least 1 km above the highest ground under the boom's path, and every 500 m of extra terrain or cushion costs about 0.004 Mach.

What about the place where it turns?

The turning point is not perfectly quiet. Where the rays flatten out they bunch together, and just below that height there is a faint rumble rather than a boom. NASA measured this in its FaINT flight tests: below the turning point the sound fades quickly with distance. That is why a safety buffer is needed between the turning point and the ground, and why the speeds on the map include a margin for forecast error. The goal is not "no sound at all" but "no boom on the ground", which is how the FAA's July 2026 proposal frames it, as a limit of 0.11 psf at the surface.

What it means for a trip

Mach cutoff does not give you Concorde's Mach 2 over land. It gives you something like Mach 1.05 to 1.3, depending on the day and the direction. That still matters. A jet at Mach 1.15 covers the ground roughly a third faster than a jet at Mach 0.85, and over the ocean the same airplane can fly as fast as it likes. In a year of past weather, an Overture-class jet using the forecast corridors at 55,000 ft saved about 25 to 28 minutes per flight on 48 routes, compared with slowing down over all land as today's rule requires.

The one equation, for the curious

If you like formulas: in layered air the quantity Vtrace = c(z) / cos θ stays the same along every ray (Snell's law). The ray turns where the local effective sound speed, c(z) + w(z), reaches the airplane's ground speed, so the boomless Mach is the largest effective sound speed below the airplane, minus the wind at the airplane, divided by the sound speed at the airplane.
