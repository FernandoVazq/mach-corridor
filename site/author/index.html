About the author

I am Fernando J. Vázquez, from Humacao, Puerto Rico. I study Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering at Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, Ohio, with Finance as a second major, and I expect to graduate in spring 2027. I also spent a semester at Universidad Carlos III de Madrid, taking thermodynamics, fluid mechanics and control systems in Spanish.

I spent the summers of 2025 and 2026 at Bell Textron in Texas. Among other projects, I built a tool that takes an aircraft's weather limits and shows where and how often around the world it can operate, and I helped plan the 500-hour operational evaluation of the Bell 525. That first tool is, in a way, where Mach Corridor began: the question of what the atmosphere allows an aircraft to do, day by day and place by place.

I am also a student pilot, training for my private pilot certificate in Cleveland and in Texas. Flying taught me to respect forecasts, and to read them with their uncertainty in mind, which is the spirit of the margins on this site.

Why I built Mach Corridor

When Boom's XB-1 flew supersonic in January 2025 without a boom reaching the ground, I wanted to know how often that is possible, where, and how fast. Nobody published an open answer, so I built one: a global forecast, checked against a year of weather balloons, with route planning and pages for rocket booms, fireballs, the X-59 and how far sound carries. I worked with Claude, an AI model by Anthropic, which wrote most of the code and ran the studies; I set the questions, read the results and made the decisions. Every number on the site comes from the public repository, where the method, the code and the list of mistakes I found and fixed are open.

Mach Corridor is a research project, not affiliated with Boom, NASA or the FAA, and not for flight planning. If you find something wrong, I would genuinely like to hear it.
