How loud is a sonic boom?

A boom is measured as the peak overpressure it puts on the ground, in pounds per square foot (psf).

PeakWhat it is like
under 0.3 psfA distant thump or rumble, like thunder a few miles away. Easy to miss indoors.
0.3 to 1 psfA clear double boom. Windows may rattle.
1 to 3 psfLoud, like close thunder. It startles people and pets; car alarms can go off.
over 3 psfVery loud. Rare outside the area right around the landing zone.

For comparison, the US rule proposed for quiet supersonic airliners limits the boom to 0.11 psf.

Why a booster landing booms on land and a launch usually does not

A rocket climbs almost straight up while it is slow. The boom leaves the vehicle on a cone, and only the part of the cone that points down can reach the ground. While the rocket climbs steeper than the cone's angle, nothing points down, so the launch boom starts late, high and far out over the ocean. A booster coming back falls nose-down toward the landing zone at two to four times the speed of sound, so its cone points down all around it and the boom lands on the towns near the base. That is the double or triple boom people hear a few minutes after liftoff.

How the forecast works

  • Atmosphere: NOAA GFS temperature and wind at the site for the forecast hour closest to liftoff, up to about 36 km, with the standard atmosphere above. Launches more than 48 hours away use the standard atmosphere until they come into forecast range; the forecast updates every 6 hours.
  • Rays: sound rays are traced from every point of the supersonic flight, in every direction on the boom cone, until they reach the ground or the atmosphere bends them back up. That bending sets the edge of the boom zone.
  • Loudness: the standard far-field scaling for a sonic boom (it falls with distance to the power 3/4), calibrated to published measurements of real Falcon 9 and Super Heavy booms.
  • Flight path: a typical path for the vehicle (altitude, speed and angle against time), the usual launch direction for the orbit, and the booster's usual approach to the landing zone. Real flights differ.

Limits

  • The loudness is an estimate, good to about a factor of 2. Hills, buildings and being indoors change what you hear.
  • Focus booms (a short, louder boom where the rays bunch up during a turn or slow-down) are not computed.
  • Launch times slip. The list comes from Launch Library 2 by The Space Devs.
